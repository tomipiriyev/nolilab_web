How Daily Ionosphere Changes Affect GPS Accuracy

The ionosphere changes between day and night. For GPS, the key is how many free electrons a signal crosses—and how unevenly they are distributed.

By Tamleykha Piriyev Published November 21, 2023 Updated October 3, 2026

The short answer: Daily changes in the ionosphere alter GPS signal travel time and can introduce positioning errors. Layer altitude influences the path through the ionosphere, but altitude alone cannot predict accuracy. The main factors are total electron content along that path, signal frequency, and small-scale irregularities. Nighttime often brings less ionization, yet disturbances after sunset can still disrupt reception.

Why altitude is only part of the story

The ionosphere is a region of the upper atmosphere where solar radiation creates free electrons and ions. Its D, E, and F regions change with sunlight and atmospheric conditions. The lower regions weaken substantially at night, while the F region persists. These are changing density regions, not solid shells moving up and down together. NOAA explains the ionosphere’s structure.

For GPS, a more useful measure is total electron content (TEC): the number of electrons along a signal’s path. Layer height affects which regions a slanted signal crosses, but a higher layer does not automatically mean a larger error. Latitude, season, solar activity, and geomagnetic conditions also change TEC. NOAA’s TEC overview explains this connection.

What changes from day to night?

Sunlight produces ionization during the day. After dark, recombination reduces electron density, although the ionosphere does not disappear. The table describes a general pattern, not a schedule for predicting GPS accuracy at a particular location.

Typical daily changes and their possible GPS effects
Local timeIonospheric changePossible GPS effect
Morning and daytimeSolar radiation replenishes free electrons.Higher electron content can increase uncorrected signal delay.
After sunsetOverall ionization falls, but equatorial irregularities can develop.Rapid fluctuations can disrupt tracking even as average electron content decreases.
NightElectron density is generally lower; the F region remains.Delay may decrease, but reliable reception is not guaranteed.

There is no universal “best GPS hour.” In particular, NOAA describes equatorial instabilities that are strongest just after sunset. A simple rule that nighttime GPS is always better would miss this effect.

How the ionosphere affects GPS measurements

1. A range error without a weak signal

GPS estimates distance from signal timing. The ionosphere delays the ranging code, making an uncorrected satellite distance appear too long. Carrier-phase measurements experience the opposite first-order effect: a phase advance. A receiver can therefore show a strong signal while its measurements still need correction.

The first-order code delay increases with TEC and decreases with the square of signal frequency. This frequency dependence is what lets dual-frequency receivers estimate and remove most of the effect. See ESA Navipedia’s ionospheric delay explanation.

2. Rapid fluctuations and loss of tracking

Small, uneven patches of electron density can cause scintillation: rapid changes in signal strength and phase. Severe scintillation can break a receiver’s lock on a satellite and interrupt position updates. It is more common at low and high latitudes than at middle latitudes. NOAA’s scintillation guide distinguishes this disruption from a smooth delay.

3. Longer paths near the horizon

A low-elevation satellite’s signal generally takes a longer route through the ionosphere than an overhead signal. Correction models account for this geometry. The same ionosphere can therefore affect different satellites by different amounts; the resulting position error also depends on their arrangement in the sky.

What helps reduce GPS errors?

  • Broadcast correction models. Single-frequency GPS receivers can use the Klobuchar model. It is designed to remove about half the ionospheric range error on a worldwide RMS basis, not a guaranteed percentage for each fix. ESA documents the model and its limits.
  • Dual-frequency measurements. Combining two frequencies removes the dominant ionospheric delay term. It does not guarantee continuous tracking during severe scintillation.
  • More usable satellites. Tracking several constellations can improve availability and geometry. Multi-constellation reception is different from dual-frequency correction: signals from every constellation still cross the ionosphere.
  • Check the whole reception environment. Give the antenna a clear view of the sky and follow its mounting guidance. Buildings, terrain, and reflections can cause problems unrelated to space weather.
  • Check conditions for precision work. Review NOAA’s space-weather information and your receiver’s quality indicators. A valid-looking coordinate alone does not show whether it meets your accuracy requirement.

What this means for Loko tracking

A tracker has two separate jobs: calculate a location from satellite signals, then deliver that location to you. Loko uses a LoRa radio link for the second job. That link does not remove an ionospheric error from a GPS fix or restore a missing satellite measurement.

If an update is missing or a location looks wrong, check both satellite reception and the tracker-to-receiver radio path. Our GPS and LoRa guide explains the two links; the Loko specifications describe the hardware’s capabilities.
