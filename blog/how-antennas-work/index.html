How Antennas Work: From Moving Electrons to Radio Waves

A simple, visual introduction to antenna physics, dipoles, and monopoles.

The short answer: An antenna turns an alternating electrical signal into a radio wave, and a radio wave back into an electrical signal. The signal moves electrons back and forth in metal, and those changing currents and charges launch an electromagnetic wave. A half-wave dipole does this with two arms and a center feed. A quarter-wave monopole uses one element and a ground plane. At 868 MHz those lengths are about 173 mm and 86 mm.

Radio can seem like magic. A small piece of metal sends energy across a valley with nothing connecting it to the receiver. But the physics behind it starts with something simple: electrons moving in a wire. This guide follows that movement step by step, from a plain wire to a working dipole antenna and monopole antenna, then back again to how an antenna receives a signal.

Start with a wire: what are electrons doing?

A metal wire is already full of electrons that can move. In copper, roughly one electron from each atom is free to wander through the metal instead of being tied to a single atom. A battery does not need to supply these electrons. They are there before you connect anything.

Connect a power source and it creates an electric field along the wire. That field pushes on every free electron, and they all begin to drift in the same direction, on top of their fast random motion.

This drift is surprisingly slow. With 1 A flowing through a copper wire with a 1 mm² cross-section, the average drift speed is only about 0.07 mm per second. A lamp still lights almost immediately because the electric field travels through the wire far faster than the electrons do, so electrons all along the wire start moving at nearly the same moment.

What makes electrons move back and forth?

A battery pushes in one direction. A radio transmitter does not. It produces an alternating voltage, one that changes smoothly, reverses polarity, and repeats.

When one end of the wire is positive, electrons are pushed toward it. When the polarity reverses, the electric force reverses too, and the electrons head the other way. They never make real progress along the wire. They just move back and forth around their local positions.

One detail often causes confusion. Electrons carry negative charge, so they move opposite to the direction we conventionally call electric current. The convention was settled before anyone knew about electrons, and it stuck. Current arrows and electron arrows therefore point in opposite directions.

Reading the wave: what does the graph actually show?

Radio signals are usually drawn as a sine wave. Four basic terms describe it:

  • Cycle: one complete repeat of the pattern, from zero to a positive peak, through zero to a negative peak, and back to zero.
  • Amplitude: how far the wave reaches from zero. A higher voltage amplitude means a stronger push on the electrons.
  • Frequency: how many cycles happen each second, measured in hertz. At 868 MHz, there are 868 million cycles every second.
  • Phase: where the wave is in its cycle at a given moment. Two waves of the same frequency can be shifted in time relative to each other.

The most important thing to remember is that the graph shows how voltage changes over time. It is not the physical path of an electron. Electrons do not wiggle up and down in the shape of a sine curve as they travel along a wire.

Electrons respond to the local electric field at their own location. In the plain wire above, voltage and current rise and fall together. An antenna is different. Its current and charge vary along its length, and at many points along a resonant antenna the current peaks a quarter of a cycle away from the local voltage. The voltage graph alone does not always tell you the exact timing of the current.

The distances are also tiny. With 1 A at 868 MHz, electrons in a typical wire move far less than the width of a single atom in each direction. A strong radio signal does not need electrons to go anywhere.

How does that movement produce a radio wave?

Three facts from physics come together here:

  1. Electrons have an electric field. Any charge has one around it, and it reaches out into the surrounding space.
  2. Electric current produces a magnetic field. Moving charges create a magnetic field around the wire.
  3. Changing fields create each other. A changing electric field produces a magnetic field, and a changing magnetic field produces an electric field.

A steady current gives a steady magnetic field, and that field simply stays around the wire. But in an antenna, currents and charges change direction millions of times per second. The fields near the antenna cannot adjust instantly, because changes spread at a finite speed. Part of the changing field keeps moving outward as a self-sustaining pattern of electric and magnetic fields. That traveling pattern is an electromagnetic wave, and it carries energy away from the antenna at the speed of light.

The electrons stay in the metal. Only the wave travels away.

Why does antenna length matter?

A wavelength, often written λ, is the distance a wave travels during one cycle. Radio waves are very fast, but at 868 MHz each cycle is extremely brief, so a wave travels only about 345 mm before the next cycle begins.

Now picture a signal moving along an antenna. It takes time to travel from the feed point to the tip, where the metal ends and it has nowhere to go, so it reflects and comes back. If the antenna has the right length, that reflection returns in step with the source. Each new push from the transmitter then adds to the motion already underway, much like pushing a swing at the right moment. This is resonance: the timing of the signal and the length of the antenna work together.

The most common lengths come from this timing. An arm a quarter wavelength long gives a round trip of half a wavelength, half a cycle later, just as the source has reversed. Two such arms make a half-wave antenna. One such arm, with a ground plane standing in for the other, makes a quarter-wave antenna.

Once the idea is clear, the arithmetic is simple. Wavelength is the speed of light divided by frequency, so in free space a quarter wavelength in meters is 75 divided by the frequency in MHz. A shorter antenna can still work, but it usually needs extra matching components and tends to be less efficient or more narrowly tuned.

The dipole antenna: two arms working together

A dipole antenna is two conducting arms connected to a signal source at the center. The source pushes electrons into one arm while pulling them out of the other, then reverses. In this way, electrons move along the arms in the same overall direction, first one way and then the other, and charge is repeatedly pushed toward the ends.

In a typical half-wave dipole, this settles into a repeating pattern called a standing wave:

  • Current is strongest near the center and falls toward zero at the tips, because electrons cannot flow past the end of the metal.
  • Charge builds up near the ends, with one tip positive and the other negative, swapping each half cycle.
  • Voltage is smallest near the center and largest at the tips. Low voltage and high current at the center give a low feed impedance, about 73 Ω in the ideal case.

The result is the familiar doughnut-shaped radiation pattern. A dipole radiates most strongly to the sides, broadside to the wire, and almost nothing directly off its ends. Concentrating energy this way gives an ideal half-wave dipole about 2.15 dBi of gain compared with a theoretical antenna that radiates equally in all directions. That gain does not add power. It only describes how the power is shared between directions.

The monopole antenna: one element and a ground plane

A quarter-wave monopole is one conducting element, a quarter wavelength long, working with a conducting surface called a ground plane. The signal is fed between the base of the element and that surface. Despite the name, a ground plane does not need to be connected to the earth.

Think of an ideal, flat, perfectly conducting ground plane. Charges in its surface rearrange in response to the element, and their effect above the plane is exactly the same as if a mirror image of the antenna existed below it. Positive charge at the tip of the element corresponds to negative charge at the tip of its reflection, and the current in the image flows in the matching direction. Put together, the element and its image have the same form as a half-wave dipole. The ground plane electrically supplies the missing half.

This has a few useful consequences for an ideal quarter-wave monopole:

  • Its radiation pattern above the plane matches the top half of a dipole's doughnut.
  • Its feed impedance is about half of a dipole's, roughly 36 Ω.
  • All the power goes into half of space, so its theoretical gain is about 3 dB higher, 5.15 dBi. Real ground planes do not reach this figure.

Real ground planes are not infinite, of course. A vehicle roof, a set of radial wires, or a circuit board can all serve, but a small or irregular surface changes the feed impedance and tends to tilt the main beam upward. The mirror model is a starting point, not an exact description of every device.

Dipole vs. monopole: a simple comparison

Here are the two designs side by side at the same frequency.

Half-wave dipole vs. quarter-wave monopole
FeatureHalf-wave dipoleQuarter-wave monopole
ConstructionTwo conducting armsOne element plus a conducting surface
Typical lengthAbout λ/2 (≈ 173 mm at 868 MHz)About λ/4 (≈ 86 mm at 868 MHz)
Feed pointCenter, between the armsBase, between element and ground plane
Ground planeNot requiredRequired, and part of the antenna
Radiation patternFull doughnutTop half of a doughnut (ideal plane)
Ideal feed impedanceAbout 73 ΩAbout 36 Ω
Ideal gain2.15 dBi5.15 dBi (infinite ground plane)

The 3 dB gain difference does not make a monopole automatically better. It comes from an idealized infinite ground plane, and a real device rarely has one. In practice, the choice usually depends on the space available, the mounting surface, and what the device is attached to.

How an antenna receives a signal

Now reverse the whole story. When an electromagnetic wave reaches a receiving antenna, its electric field pushes on the free electrons in the metal. As the field alternates, the electrons move back and forth, producing a small alternating voltage and current at the antenna's feed point. A receiver amplifies and decodes that signal.

Thanks to a property called reciprocity, an antenna generally has the same radiation pattern and gain whether it is sending or receiving. A dipole that transmits poorly off its ends also receives poorly from that direction.

The received signal can be astonishingly small. Loko's LoRa receiver sensitivity of −137 dBm is about 0.00000000000000002 W, which corresponds to roughly 30 billionths of a volt across a 50 Ω input. It's a good illustration of why a well-placed antenna matters so much.

What this means for LoRa and GPS trackers

A LoRa link and a GPS receiver both rely on these same principles, though at different frequencies. Higher frequencies mean shorter wavelengths and smaller antennas:

Calculated free-space lengths
SignalWavelengthHalf-waveQuarter-wave
LoRa, 868 MHz (EU)≈ 345 mm≈ 173 mm≈ 86 mm
LoRa, 915 MHz (US)≈ 328 mm≈ 164 mm≈ 82 mm
GPS L1, 1575.42 MHz≈ 190 mm≈ 95 mm≈ 48 mm

A tiny tracker cannot always fit an ideal 86 mm straight wire, and it has no large metal sheet underneath. Compact designs often use coiled, folded, or chip antennas, and rely on the circuit board and battery as their conducting surface. GPS antennas also face a different job, since GPS satellites transmit a circularly polarized signal, so many receivers use small patch antennas instead of a simple wire.

Whatever the shape, the same physics gives some practical advice for getting better range from a tracker:

  • Mind the orientation. With a dipole-like pattern, the weakest direction is off the ends of the antenna. Pointing a whip straight at the other radio is usually worse than holding it upright.
  • Match polarization. For the best signal, keep linearly polarized antennas at both ends oriented the same way, typically both vertical.
  • Keep antennas clear of metal and bodies. Nearby conductors and tissue can detune an antenna and absorb energy, because they become part of its electrical surroundings.
  • Height helps. Antenna design is only one part of a radio link. See how radio line of sight and distance affect signal range.
  • Don't assume a bigger antenna is better. An antenna tuned for the wrong band can perform worse than a smaller one tuned for the right band.

Frequently asked questions

What is the simplest explanation of how an antenna works?

An alternating signal moves electrons back and forth in a piece of metal. Those changing currents and charges create an electromagnetic wave that travels away at the speed of light. To receive, the process runs in reverse: an arriving wave moves electrons in the metal and creates a small electrical signal.

What is the difference between a dipole and a monopole antenna?

A dipole has two conducting arms fed at the center and does not need a ground plane. A monopole has one element fed at its base and uses a conducting surface, called a ground plane, in place of the second arm. A quarter-wave monopole is half the length of a half-wave dipole at the same frequency.

How long should a quarter-wave antenna be?

A quarter wavelength in free space is 75 divided by the frequency in MHz, giving meters. That is about 86 mm at 868 MHz and about 82 mm at 915 MHz. Real antennas are usually a few percent shorter and tuned for their particular surroundings.

Does a monopole antenna need a ground plane?

Yes, it needs something conducting to work against. This can be a metal sheet, a vehicle roof, radial wires, or the circuit board and battery of a small device. A small or uneven ground plane still works, but it changes the tuning and radiation pattern.

Is a dipole or a monopole antenna better?

Neither is better everywhere. A dipole works well when you have room and no convenient ground plane. A monopole is shorter and suits products and vehicles that already have a conducting surface. Placement and tuning often matter more than the name of the antenna.

Do electrons travel from the transmitting antenna to the receiving antenna?

No. Electrons stay in the metal of each antenna. Only the electromagnetic wave crosses the space between them, carrying energy and information.
