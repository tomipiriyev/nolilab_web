GPS vs GSM vs LoRa: How Tracking Actually Works

GPS calculates a position. Cellular networks or LoRa radio carry that position to you.

A tracker can know where it is while your phone shows an old location. The missing piece is often the connection between the tracker and you.

To understand why, separate two jobs: finding a location and sending it somewhere. GPS handles the first. GSM and other cellular technologies, or a LoRa radio link, can handle the second. The connection you choose determines where you can receive updates, what equipment you need, and whether a service plan is involved.

GPS Finds the Position

A GPS receiver listens to satellite signals and uses their timing to calculate its position. It does not need a SIM card or mobile data connection to do that, and it does not report its location back to the GPS satellites.

GPS is one satellite navigation system. GNSS is the broader term that also includes systems such as Galileo and GLONASS. A tracker described as a “GPS tracker” may use several of these systems.

Reception matters. Buildings, dense tree cover, and terrain can obstruct signals or reduce accuracy. A working communication link cannot fix a missing position: the tracker may deliver a message successfully without having a fresh location to send. GPS.gov explains the factors that affect positioning accuracy.

GSM and Cellular Networks Send the Update

In a typical cellular tracker, the GPS receiver calculates a position, then a cellular modem sends it through a mobile network to a service that your app can access. You can be far from the tracker and still see updates, provided the tracker has supported coverage and your phone can reach the service.

Strictly speaking, GSM refers to a 2G mobile network standard. Modern cellular trackers may instead use LTE, LTE-M, or NB-IoT. These names are not interchangeable: the device must support the network available where you use it. Before buying an older GSM tracker, check the operator's support plans; 2G and 3G network retirements make that especially relevant.

Cellular connectivity usually has a service cost. It may appear as a monthly subscription, prepaid credit, or service bundled into the purchase price. An app or cloud platform can add its own fee. The charge pays for connectivity and services, rather than access to GPS signals.

LoRa Sends Small Packets over a Radio Link

Location updates contain little data, so they suit a radio designed for small messages. LoRa provides that radio link. In a point-to-point setup, a tracker sends coordinates directly to a compatible receiver nearby, without routing them through a cellular operator.

The receiver is essential. A standard phone cannot receive LoRa packets by itself; it needs compatible hardware to pass the data to an app. You also need to stay within the link's working range. Hills, buildings, vegetation, antenna placement, and radio settings all affect whether packets arrive.

Semtech describes LoRa as a low-power, long-range radio technology. Those properties do not guarantee a particular distance or battery life for a finished tracker. Frequent GPS fixes and frequent transmissions still consume energy.

LoRa and LoRaWAN are different parts of a system

LoRa is the radio technology; LoRaWAN is a networking protocol built on it. A LoRaWAN deployment uses gateways and a network server, while a direct point-to-point link can operate between compatible devices. A product saying “LoRa” does not, by itself, tell you whether it needs a gateway, internet access, or a paid network service. See Semtech's LoRa and LoRaWAN overview for the architecture.

What Happens When the Connection Drops?

Imagine a tracker on a dog moving behind a hill. Its GPS receiver may still calculate a fresh position, but the hill could block the radio path to your receiver. Your app then has an older received location, not proof of where the dog is now.

A cellular tracker has the same separation between positioning and delivery: outside supported network coverage, it may know its coordinates but be unable to send them. Some devices store positions and upload them later; check whether the specific tracker supports that behavior.

When reading a map, check the timestamp as well as the marker. For offline use, also check whether the app can display saved maps. Receiving coordinates without internet and downloading map tiles are separate capabilities.

Which Connection Fits Your Use?

Cellular fits tracking across a wide area with supported coverage. It can suit a vehicle or asset that travels far beyond your own receiver's reach. Check the exact network technology, coverage, update interval, and ongoing service cost.

Direct LoRa fits local tracking where you can keep a receiver within reach. It can suit fieldwork, a farm, or equipment recovery without mobile service. Test the link in the actual terrain: a clear, elevated path and a route behind a ridge can give very different results.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does GPS work without a SIM card?

Yes. A GPS receiver can calculate its position without a SIM card or internet connection when it has usable satellite signals. Sending that position to someone else requires a separate communication link.

Is GSM the same as GPS?

No. GPS calculates a position from satellite signals. GSM is a 2G cellular communication standard that a tracker can use to send data. Newer cellular trackers may use other network technologies.

Can my phone receive LoRa directly?

A standard smartphone does not have a built-in LoRa radio. A direct LoRa tracking setup needs a compatible receiver that passes the location data to the phone.

Does LoRa tracking always have no subscription?

A direct LoRa point-to-point link does not require a cellular plan. Network services, apps, or cloud features may have separate fees, so check the complete product and service package.

Does LoRa make GPS more accurate?

No. In a GPS-plus-LoRa tracker, the GPS receiver calculates the coordinates and LoRa transports them. The radio link affects whether an update arrives, not the accuracy of that GPS fix.

Can I track a LoRa device from anywhere?

With a direct point-to-point setup, you need a compatible receiver within radio range. Remote access through gateways or an internet-connected service requires additional infrastructure.
