Long Range GPS Tracker With No Monthly Fee: How Far It Really Reaches

Working out a position is free. Getting it to you is where range, and fees, come from.

Every GPS tracker works out its position from satellites, and that part costs nothing. What decides how far away you can see it, and whether you pay every month, is how the position travels back to you. Trackers that reach you from anywhere borrow a mobile or satellite network and charge a plan for it. Trackers with no fee send the position over a radio link you own, so their range is the range of that radio. This page compares the types by the makers' own range claims and fees, explains why line of sight decides radio range, and says plainly where Loko fits.

Updated 24 November 2026. Tractive and Dogtra prices checked on the makers' websites on that date; Garmin prices checked on 13 November 2026. Prices change, so check again before you buy.

Two Kinds of Range

  • Network range: a cellular or satellite tracker reaches you wherever its network reaches the tracker. Distance between you and the tracker doesn't matter; coverage does. You pay a plan.
  • Radio range: a tracker that talks straight to a receiver you carry only reaches as far as that radio link. There is no network to pay for, but distance, terrain and height matter.

Why Line of Sight Decides Radio Range

Radio trackers reach furthest when the two ends can “see” each other. Over flat ground the curve of the earth hides a low antenna surprisingly soon, so height matters more than almost anything else. The table below uses the standard radio-horizon formula for smooth, flat ground with nothing in the way. It is an illustration, not a range promise: real ground has trees, hills and buildings, which shorten range further.

So long open-area ranges, such as Loko's 20+ km, need clear line of sight and usually one end up high, such as the receiver on a ridge or a tracker on a drone. Down in a valley or a forest, expect much less.

Check Range Before You Rely on It

  1. Test on your own ground, with the tracker where it will really be: on the collar, the drone or the gear.
  2. Walk or drive away and note where updates stop, especially behind hills and in trees.
  3. Find the high spots where you can stand or place the receiver to see more of the area.
  4. Set an update interval that suits the job and note how fast it uses the battery.

How Loko Works

The 14 g Loko Air gets its position from GPS and sends it over LoRa radio to a Loko Ground you keep with you. The Ground passes it to the Loko app on your phone by Bluetooth. No SIM, no mobile network and no plan are involved, and one Ground can follow up to 30 trackers. You set the radio frequency yourself; see the LoRa frequency guide.

When the Air is out of range, the app keeps the last position it received with the time it arrived, but shows no warning that it is old. The Air keeps logging up to 3,100 positions, which you can read later over USB. If the Air can't get a fix within 60 seconds, it sends its last known position. Loko does not relay positions through other trackers.

Which Type Fits You?

  1. Will you be near the tracker, or far away from it?
  2. Is there mobile coverage where the tracker goes?
  3. Do you need SOS or messaging?
  4. How many dogs, drones or pieces of gear do you track?
  5. Will the tracker get wet?
  6. How often do you need a new position?

Near the tracker, often without coverage, several things to track, dry conditions: a radio tracker like Loko fits. Far away, or needing SOS: pick a cellular or satellite tracker and budget for the plan.

When Loko Is the Wrong Choice

  • Tracking from far away: beyond radio range of your Ground, you only see the last position.
  • Emergencies: Loko has no SOS and no messaging. Use a beacon or satellite communicator.
  • Water: Loko is not waterproof; light rain only.
  • Fast updates for days: battery life depends on the update interval, up to 12 months at long intervals and much less at short ones.
  • Certified equipment: Loko holds no certifications, so check your local radio rules for the frequency you set.

FAQ

Can a long range GPS tracker have no monthly fee?

Yes, if it sends its position over a radio link you own, such as a LoRa tracker with its own receiver or a GPS dog collar with a handheld. Trackers that reach you from anywhere use a mobile or satellite network, and that is what the fee pays for.

How far does Loko reach?

20+ km in open areas with line of sight between the Loko Air and the Loko Ground, and much less among trees, hills and buildings. Test the range on your own ground before you rely on it.

Why do range claims vary so much?

Range depends mostly on line of sight. Two radios held low over flat ground lose sight of each other over the curve of the earth after roughly 8 to 10 km, and trees, hills and buildings cut that further. Long claims assume open ground and at least one end up high.

What happens when the tracker goes out of range?

The Loko app keeps the last position it received with the time it arrived, but shows no warning that it is old, so check the time. The Air keeps logging up to 3,100 positions, which you can read later over USB.

Does Loko need a SIM, internet or a subscription?

No. The Loko Air sends its position over LoRa radio to the Loko Ground, and the Ground passes it to the Loko app on your phone by Bluetooth. There is no SIM, no mobile network and no plan.

Loko tracks a dog, drone or gear within radio range with no SIM and no monthly fee.
